Passwords

The vault

Encrypted, and the key never touches the disk

One file, coffre.json, encrypted with AES-256-GCM using a key derived from the master password by PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA512, 210,000 rounds. The key lives in memory and disappears on locking.

One byte changed, one file refused

GCM authenticates as much as it encrypts: a modified vault is refused instead of returning anything.

Locked at launch

It never opens by force: on a sign-in page, autofill offers “Unlock the vault…”, right where it's useful.

Locked again after signing in

Opened with your finger to sign in, it locks again as soon as the password is in the page — or after two minutes if it served no purpose. Opened from the settings, it waits until you're done.

Locked along with the Mac

Screen locked, sleep, session switched to another account: the key leaves memory, and Touch ID gives it back when you return.

What you lose, said plainly

No iCloud sync and no keychain backup. And a forgotten master password is a lost vault: that's what “encrypted” means — hence typing it twice when creating it.

Touch ID

A shortcut, not a replacement

The vault opens with your finger if you turn it on. The master password stays valid: adding a fingerprint to the Mac invalidates the stored key, and without it the vault would become impossible to open.

Thirty-two bytes, in the Enclave

What's handed to the Secure Enclave is the vault's key, useless without the file. Never a login, never a site name. Turning off Touch ID erases the stored key.

Autofill

Under the field you clicked

The list of accounts known for this site, filtered as you type — case and accents ignored. ↑ ↓ ↵ choose, Escape closes.

A choice only fills that field

Choosing an account in the username field doesn't take the password out of the vault: it only leaves when its own field was picked.

The page never sees the list

It's drawn by macOS, not inside the page: a site can neither read it nor count your accounts. And it only carries names.

Two-step sign-ins

Google, Microsoft and others ask for the address first: the field declared “username” is enough to offer, and filling writes the address alone.

Instant

The vault is decrypted once and indexed by site: 0.2 µs per keystroke over two thousand logins.

Three rules

The host comes from WebKit, never from the page

It's the frame's real origin that speaks: a page can't pretend to be your bank and receive its login.

Nothing without a yes

Saving is offered, not done. Filling is only automatic when there's nothing to choose; from two accounts on, you choose.

HTTPS, except at home

A password isn't offered in clear text over the network; a service hosted on your own machine — localhost, .local, private networks — crosses none.

In and out

Import

No third-party browser can read the Passwords app. Its CSV export — or Chrome's, Firefox's, Bitwarden's, 1Password's — is read in Settings › Passwords, and each line goes in encrypted. How many go in and how many are set aside is stated before writing.

Export

With the same columns: what leaves here reads back in Safari, Chrome or Firefox. An encrypted export only Wuji could read wouldn't be an export — it's in clear text, and it says so. That's why it always asks for Touch ID or the master password again, even with the vault open, and why the file is readable by you alone.

Copying without going through the page

Settings › Passwords lists the host and the account, never the secret. “Copy” puts it on the clipboard without it passing through the page; it doesn't leave this Mac, declares itself secret, and is cleared after 90 seconds.

HTTP authentication

A server asking who you are opens a bubble at the bottom right. Nothing is kept there: what you type goes to the server and disappears with it. Only the site shown can ask: an image from another site can't invite itself over your bank.
